Topic 1: Electric Charges & Static Electricity

1. Atomic Structure & Charge Movement

  • Protons (+): Tightly locked in atomic nucleus with neutrons. Protons NEVER move during charging of solids.
  • Electrons (-): Orbit the nucleus; outer (valence) electrons are mobile and transfer between objects.
  • Law of Charges: Like charges repel; opposite charges attract; charged objects attract neutral objects (via induction).
  • Conservation of Charge: Total electric charge in an isolated system is strictly conserved.

2. Three Methods of Charging

  • Friction: Rubbing two dissimilar materials; electrons transfer to the material with higher electron affinity (e.g. rubber rod captures electrons from fur).
  • Conduction (Contact): Direct physical contact between charged object and conductor; charge is shared.
  • Induction: Charged object brought near neutral object without touching, temporarily repelling like charges and attracting opposite charges (polarization).

3. Conductors, Insulators & Semiconductors

  • Conductors: Valence electrons move freely (copper, silver, gold, aluminum). Excess charge distributes evenly across exterior.
  • Insulators: Electrons tightly bound, non-mobile (rubber, plastic, glass). Holds static charge localized in place.
  • Semiconductors: Intermediate conductivity (silicon, selenium). Selenium insulates in darkness and conducts when exposed to light (used in photocopiers).
  • Superconductors: Materials with zero resistance at cryogenic temperatures.

4. Static Applications & Grounding

  • Electrostatic Spray Painting: Paint mist given negative charge; car body grounded/positive → attracts paint evenly, minimizes overspray.
  • Electrostatic Precipitator: Smoke particles charged negatively and collected on positive plates in smokestacks.
  • Grounding: Connecting a conductor to Earth; Earth absorbs or supplies electrons safely to neutralize static charge.
  • Lightning Rods: Pointed metal rods conducting lightning safely around buildings into the earth.

Topic 2: Current Electricity & Circuit Fundamentals

1. Static vs. Current Electricity

  • Static: Stationary charge buildup resulting in sudden uncontrolled discharge (brief spark).
  • Current: Continuous, steady flow of electrons through a complete closed circuit loop.
  • Water Analogy: Voltage = water pump pressure; Current = water flow rate; Resistor = constriction in pipe; Switch = valve.

2. Conventional Current vs. Electron Flow

  • Conventional Current: Benjamin Franklin's historical model: positive fluid flows from Positive (+) to Negative (-). Still used on circuit diagrams!
  • Electron Flow: Physical reality in solid conductors: negative electrons flow from Negative (-) to Positive (+).

3. 4 Circuit Elements & Meters

  • Source: Provides energy/potential difference (cell, battery, generator).
  • Conductor: Conducting pathway (copper wire).
  • Load: Device converting electrical energy into heat, light, sound, or mechanical work.
  • Control: Switch that opens (breaks) or closes (completes) the circuit.
  • Ammeter: Measures Current ($I$). Connected in SERIES (low internal resistance).
  • Voltmeter: Measures Voltage ($V$). Connected in PARALLEL across load (high internal resistance).
  • Galvanometer: Detects and measures very weak electric currents and direction.

4. Drift Speed vs. Electric Field

  • Drift Speed: Individual electrons drift slowly (~1 meter per hour) in a chaotic zigzag collision path with wire atoms.
  • Electric Field Signal: Travels at nearly the speed of light through the entire circuit instantly, causing electrons everywhere to start moving simultaneously.

Topic 3: Resistance, Ohm's Law & Circuit Analysis

1. 4 Factors Affecting Resistance

  • Material: Copper/silver have low resistivity; Nichrome/tungsten have high resistivity.
  • Thickness: Thicker wire = lower resistance (wider pipe permits more electrons to pass).
  • Length: Longer wire = higher resistance (more collisions along the wire).
  • Temperature: Hotter wire = higher resistance (thermal vibration increases collisions).

2. Resistor Types

  • Fixed Resistors: Constant resistance used to limit current.
  • Variable Resistors (Rheostats): Adjustable resistance (dimmer switches, volume knobs, stove dials).
  • Ohmic Resistors: Constant resistance independent of voltage; produces a straight linear $V$ vs $I$ graph.
  • Non-Ohmic: Resistance varies with temperature/voltage (e.g. light bulb filament; curved graph).

3. Series Circuits

  • Single conducting loop: $I_{total} = I_1 = I_2 = I_3$ (current is the SAME everywhere).
  • Voltage divides: $V_{total} = V_1 + V_2 + V_3$.
  • Total resistance increases: $R_{total} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3$.
  • Adding loads decreases current and dims all bulbs; one burnout breaks entire circuit.

4. Parallel Circuits

  • Multiple independent branches: $V_{total} = V_1 = V_2 = V_3$ (voltage is the SAME across branches).
  • Current divides: $I_{total} = I_1 + I_2 + I_3$.
  • Total resistance DECREASES: $1/R_{total} = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2 + 1/R_3$.
  • Adding branches does NOT dim existing bulbs; one burnout leaves other branches running (used in house wiring).

Topic 4: The Energy Connection (Transformations & Devices)

1. Forms of Energy & Laws of Thermodynamics

  • Energy: The ability to do work (Joules). Forms: Chemical, Electrical, Mechanical, Thermal, Nuclear, Light.
  • 1st Law of Thermodynamics: Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed from one form to another.
  • 2nd Law of Thermodynamics: No energy transformation is 100% efficient; some energy is always dissipated as waste thermal energy (heat).

2. Thermocouples & Thermopiles

  • Thermocouple: Device of two different metals joined at ends. When one junction is heated and the other is cold, heat generates an electric current (Thermal → Electrical).
  • Used as temperature sensors in extreme heat (kilns, furnaces, jet engines).
  • Thermopile: Multiple thermocouples connected in series to produce higher voltage.

3. Piezoelectric Effect

  • Piezoelectric Effect: Applying mechanical stress/pressure to a quartz crystal generates an electric voltage (Mechanical → Electrical). Used in BBQ spark igniters, crystal microphones.
  • Reverse Piezoelectric Effect: Applying voltage causes crystal to rapidly expand/contract (Electrical → Mechanical). Used in quartz watches, ultrasound machines, speakers.

4. Photovoltaic Cells & LEDs

  • Photovoltaic (PV) Solar Cells: Made of semiconducting silicon. Sunlight knocks electrons free, creating DC electricity (Light → Electrical).
  • LED (Light-Emitting Diode): Semiconductor converting current directly into light with minimal heat (Electrical → Light). Far more efficient than incandescent bulbs.

Topic 5: Portable Power (Electrochemical Cells)

1. Electrochemical Cell Components

  • Converts Chemical Energy → Electrical Energy.
  • Two Different Metal Electrodes: Must be different metals so one has greater electron affinity. One oxidizes (loses electrons, negative anode) and the other reduces (positive cathode).
  • Electrolyte: Liquid or paste containing ions that conducts charge between electrodes (acid, base, or salt solution).

2. Wet Cells vs. Dry Cells

  • Wet Cell: Liquid electrolyte (usually sulfuric acid). Example: 12 V Lead-Acid car battery (six 2.0 V wet cells in series). Heavy, corrosive liquid, but delivers huge starter current.
  • Dry Cell: Moist paste, plastic or solid electrolyte. Sealed, portable, leak-proof. Examples: Zinc-carbon, Alkaline batteries.

3. Primary vs. Secondary Cells

  • Primary Cell: Non-rechargeable. Chemical reaction is irreversible; once active chemicals are consumed, the cell is dead. (Zinc-carbon, standard alkaline).
  • Secondary Cell: Rechargeable. Chemical reactions can be reversed by forcing electric current backwards through the cell. (Lead-acid, NiMH, Lithium-ion).

4. Cell Stacking, Fuel Cells & Electrolysis

  • Battery Definition: A collection of two or more electrochemical cells connected in series. A 9 V battery contains six 1.5 V cells in series.
  • Fuel Cell: Converts chemical energy of continuous fuel (usually hydrogen + oxygen) directly into electricity and water without combustion. Clean, high efficiency.
  • Electrolysis: Using electricity to drive non-spontaneous chemical reactions (e.g. splitting water into $H_2$ and $O_2$; electroplating jewelry).

Topic 6: Generators and Motors (Electromagnetism)

1. Oersted & Electromagnetism

  • Hans Christian Oersted (1820): Discovered that electric current flowing through a wire creates a magnetic field around it.
  • Electromagnet: Coil of wire (solenoid) wrapped around a soft iron core. Magnetic only when current flows.
  • To Strengthen an Electromagnet: 1) Increase number of wire coils; 2) Increase current; 3) Use a ferromagnetic (iron) core.

2. Faraday & Electromagnetic Induction

  • Michael Faraday (1831): Moving a conducting wire through a magnetic field (or changing magnetic field inside a coil) induces an electric current.
  • This is the fundamental principle of all electric Generators!

3. Motors vs. Generators

  • Electric Generator: Converts Mechanical Energy → Electrical Energy (spinning turbine turns coil in magnetic field).
  • Electric Motor: Converts Electrical Energy → Mechanical Energy (current in coil interacts with magnets to spin armature).
  • They have identical parts; one is simply the reverse of the other!

4. DC Motor Components

  • Armature: Rotating wire coil that becomes an electromagnet.
  • Permanent Magnets: Provide external magnetic field pushing the armature.
  • Split-Ring Commutator: Reverses the direction of current in the armature coil every half-rotation to keep it spinning continuously in one direction.
  • Brushes: Stationary carbon/graphite contacts connecting power source to rotating commutator rings.
  • AC vs DC: DC flows in one direction; AC alternates back and forth. Power plants use AC because AC voltage is easily transformed.

Topic 7: Electricity in the Home & Efficiency

1. Power & Energy Calculations

  • Power ($P = IV$): Measured in Watts (W). Rate of energy consumption.
  • Energy in Joules ($E = Pt$): Power in Watts multiplied by time in seconds.
  • Kilowatt-Hour ($kWh$): Commercial energy unit. $E(kWh) = P(kW) imes t(hours)$. Used on home utility bills because 1 Joule is too tiny.

2. Percent Efficiency

  • $\% ext{ Efficiency} = rac{ ext{Useful Energy Output}}{ ext{Total Energy Input}} imes 100$.
  • Incandescent lightbulb: ~5% to 12% efficient (88-95% wasted as heat).
  • LED bulb: ~80-90% efficient. Electric motor: ~80-95% efficient.
  • Efficiency is always < 100% due to friction and heat dissipation (2nd Law).

3. Transformers (Step-Up vs Step-Down)

  • Use electromagnetic induction with AC current to change voltage.
  • Step-Up Transformer: Increases voltage (and decreases current) at power plants for efficient high-voltage long-distance transmission ($100,000+ V$).
  • Step-Down Transformer: Decreases voltage down to safe household levels ($240 V / 120 V$).

4. Electrical Safety Devices

  • Short Circuit: Accidental low-resistance connection between conductors causing massive current spike, extreme heating, and fires.
  • Fuse: Contains thin metal strip that melts when current exceeds rating. One-time use; must be replaced.
  • Circuit Breaker: Bimetallic strip or electromagnet switch that trips open when current is excessive. Resettable.
  • Three-Prong Plug: Round 3rd prong connects metal appliance chassis to the building ground wire, safely diverting fault current into the earth.
  • Physiological Effects: 1 mA = tingling; 15-20 mA = muscle lockup ("cannot let go"); 50-100 mA = ventricular fibrillation & fatal cardiac arrest.

Topic 8: Electricity Generation, Environmental Trade-Offs & Alberta Context

1. Thermal Generation Process

  • Standard thermal power plants: Fuel burned → Boils water into high-pressure Steam → Steam drives Turbine → Turbine spins wire coils in Generator → Electricity → Transformers → Grid.
  • Used in Coal, Natural Gas, Nuclear, Biomass, and Geothermal plants.

2. Alberta Energy Transition

  • Historically, Alberta relied primarily on burning coal for electricity.
  • Alberta completed its phase-out of coal ahead of schedule, transitioning to Natural Gas as its primary baseload source.
  • Southern Alberta is expanding rapidly in Wind and Solar energy (Canada's sunniest/windiest corridor).

3. Environmental Impacts

  • Greenhouse Gases: $CO_2$ from combustion traps heat, causing global climate change.
  • Acid Rain: Sulfur dioxide ($SO_2$) and nitrogen oxides ($NO_x$) from coal/fossil fuels react with atmospheric moisture to form sulfuric and nitric acids.
  • Thermal Pollution: Warm cooling water dumped into rivers/lakes decreases dissolved oxygen, harming aquatic ecosystems.
  • Nuclear Waste: Spent fuel rods remain radioactive and toxic for thousands of years; deep geological disposal challenges.
  • Strip Mining: Destroys surface habitats and soil structure during coal/uranium extraction.

4. Sustainable Solutions & Cogeneration

  • Cogeneration (Combined Heat and Power): Using waste heat from electricity generation to heat industrial buildings or processes (doubles overall efficiency!).
  • Renewable Energy: Wind, solar, hydroelectric, geothermal, and tidal resources replenish naturally.
  • Nuclear Fission vs Fusion: Fission splits heavy atoms (uranium; creates radioactive waste); Fusion joins light hydrogen atoms (clean, huge energy, but experimental).
