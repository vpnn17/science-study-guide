Topic 1: Electric Charges & Static Electricity

1. The Atom & Only Electrons Move

  • Protons (+): Positive charge, locked rigidly inside nucleus with neutrons. Protons NEVER move during charging in solids!
  • Neutrons (0): Neutral, located in nucleus.
  • Electrons (-): Negative charge, orbit in electron shells. Outer (valence) electrons can be transferred from atom to atom.
  • Law of Charges: Like charges repel; opposite charges attract; charged objects attract neutral objects (via induction).
  • Conservation of Charge: Total net charge of an isolated system remains constant.

2. Three Methods of Charging

  • Friction: Two different neutral materials rubbed together. One material with higher electron affinity captures electrons from the other (e.g. wool rubs balloon → balloon gets negative, wool positive).
  • Conduction (Contact): Charged object touches neutral object. Direct physical transfer of electrons until charge is shared.
  • Induction: Charged object is brought close to a neutral object without touching. Causes electrons to shift (polarization/separation of charge). Leaves object temporarily charged unless grounded.

3. Conductors vs. Insulators vs. Semiconductors

  • Conductors: Valence electrons move freely (copper, silver, gold, aluminum, saltwater). Excess charge distributes evenly across exterior.
  • Insulators: Electrons tightly bound, cannot flow freely (rubber, glass, plastic, dry wood). Holds static charge localized in place.
  • Semiconductors: Moderate conductivity (e.g., silicon, selenium). Selenium conducts light and insulates in the dark (used in photocopiers).
  • Superconductors: Zero electrical resistance at extreme low temperatures.

4. Static Applications & Safety Hazards

  • Electrostatic Spray Painting: Paint mist given negative charge; car body grounded/positive → attracts evenly, minimizes overspray.
  • Electrostatic Precipitator: Smoke particles given negative charge, attracted to positive plates in smokestacks.
  • Grounding: Connecting an object to Earth with a conductor; Earth acts as a massive reservoir that absorbs or supplies electrons.
  • Hazards: Sparks at gas stations, electronic damage, lightning. Lightning rods and airplane static wicks safely dissipate charge.

Topic 2: Current Electricity & Circuit Fundamentals

1. Current Electricity vs. Static

  • Static Electricity: Charges build up and remain stationary until sudden discharge (brief spark/shock). Cannot power steady loads.
  • Current Electricity: Continuous, controlled flow of electric charges through a complete closed circuit loop.
  • Water Analogy: Voltage = water pump pressure; Current = volume rate of water flow; Wires = pipes; Resistor = narrow pipe constriction.

2. Conventional Current vs. Electron Flow

  • Conventional Current: Proposed by Benjamin Franklin before electrons were discovered. Assumed positive fluid flows from Positive (+) to Negative (-). Still used in standard engineering diagrams!
  • Electron Flow: Physical reality in solid conductors: negatively charged electrons flow from Negative (-) to Positive (+).

3. 4 Circuit Elements & Meter Hookups

  • Source: Supplies energy (battery, cell, generator).
  • Conductor: Pathway for electrons (copper wires).
  • Load: Converts electrical energy into other forms (bulb = light/heat, motor = mechanical, heater = thermal).
  • Control: Switch that opens (breaks) or closes (completes) circuit.
  • Ammeter: Measures Current ($I$ in Amps). ALWAYS hooked in SERIES (low internal resistance).
  • Voltmeter: Measures Potential Difference ($V$ in Volts). ALWAYS hooked in PARALLEL across load/source (high internal resistance).

4. Drift Speed vs. Electric Field Speed

  • Electron Drift Speed: Individual electrons drift slowly (~1 meter per hour) in a chaotic zigzag collision path.
  • Electric Field Signal: Travels at nearly the speed of light through the entire circuit instantly when switch closes, commanding all electrons to start marching simultaneously!

Topic 3: Resistance, Ohm's Law & Circuit Types

4 Factors Affecting Conductor Resistance
1. Material (Resistivity)
Different metals hold electrons differently. Copper/silver = low R; Nichrome/tungsten = high R.
2. Thickness / Cross-Section
Thicker wire = LOWER resistance (wider pipe allows more charges through at once).
3. Length of Wire
Longer wire = HIGHER resistance (more collisions between electrons and atoms).
4. Temperature
Hotter = HIGHER resistance (atoms vibrate vigorously, impeding electron drift). Colder = lower R.
